What microfinance is — and what it isn’t.

Microfinance is one of the most discussed ideas in international development, and one of the most frequently oversold. Here is what it actually consists of, what the research has found, and where the serious criticisms lie.

The basics

A definition worth being precise about

Microfinance is the provision of financial services — loans, savings, insurance, payments — to people who are excluded from the conventional banking system, typically in amounts far too small for a commercial bank to find worth its while.

The word most people actually mean is microcredit: small loans, often between $100 and $2,000, made to someone running a small enterprise. A seamstress who needs to buy fabric in bulk. A vendor who wants to add a second cart. A farmer covering seed costs between harvests. These are people with a business and a plan, whose only obstacle is that no institution will lend them a few hundred dollars.

The central insight is unglamorous: the poor are not unbankable because they do not repay. They are unbankable because the fixed cost of assessing, issuing, and collecting a $300 loan is roughly the same as for a $300,000 one, and no bank can make that arithmetic work. Microfinance is, in large part, a set of techniques for driving that cost down.

Microcredit
A small loan extended to an individual or group that lacks access to conventional bank credit. The defining feature is size, not purpose.
Group lending
Borrowers form a small group and are jointly accountable for repayment. Peer knowledge substitutes for the credit checks a bank cannot afford to run.
Field partner
A local lending institution that identifies borrowers, disburses funds, and collects repayments on the ground. Platforms such as Kiva work through these partners in most countries.
The unbanked
Adults with no account at a bank, other financial institution, or mobile money provider. As of 2024, roughly 1.3 billion people worldwide.
Portfolio at risk
The share of a lender’s outstanding loans that are overdue. The standard measure of whether a microfinance portfolio is healthy.

Where it came from

Bangladesh, 1976

Muhammad Yunus, then an economics professor at Chittagong University, lent the equivalent of $27 from his own pocket to a group of forty-two villagers in Jobra who were making bamboo furniture and losing nearly all their profit to moneylenders. Every one of them repaid.

The experiment became the Grameen Bank, formally established in 1983, built on an idea that ran against banking orthodoxy: that people without collateral, without credit history, and without literacy could be reliable borrowers if the lending model was designed around their circumstances rather than a bank’s.

In 2006 Yunus and Grameen Bank shared the Nobel Peace Prize. By then the model had been copied, adapted, commercialised, and criticised across every continent.

  • 1976 — The first loans

    Yunus lends $27 of his own money to forty-two villagers in Jobra, Bangladesh.

  • 1983 — Grameen Bank founded

    The project becomes an independent bank, lending primarily to women in rural Bangladesh.

  • 1990s — Global replication

    The model spreads across South Asia, Africa, and Latin America, in both nonprofit and commercial forms.

  • 2005 — Kiva launches

    Person-to-person lending arrives online, letting individuals fund specific borrowers in $25 increments.

  • 2006 — Nobel Peace Prize

    Awarded jointly to Yunus and Grameen Bank “for their efforts to create economic and social development from below”.

  • 2010 — The Andhra Pradesh crisis

    Aggressive commercial lending and over-indebtedness in the Indian state trigger a sector-wide reckoning about borrower protection.

  • 2015 — The evidence arrives

    Six randomised evaluations across four continents are published together, sharply narrowing what can honestly be claimed.

Mechanics

How a microloan actually moves

The chain between a lender in one country and a borrower in another is shorter than most people expect, but it has real intermediaries, and each one matters.

  1. A borrower applies locally

    Usually through a field partner — a local microfinance institution, cooperative, or nonprofit that knows the community and can assess the request.

  2. The loan is listed

    On Kiva, the request appears publicly with the borrower’s name, business, amount requested, and what the money is for.

  3. Lenders crowdfund it in increments

    Many lenders contribute $25 or more until the loan is fully funded. No single lender carries the whole request.

  1. Funds are disbursed

    In most cases the field partner has already advanced the money locally, and crowdfunding replenishes its capital so it can lend again.

  2. The borrower repays on a schedule

    Typically in small, frequent instalments over six to eighteen months, matched to the cash flow of the business.

  3. Repayments return to the lender

    The money lands back in the lending account with no interest earned, ready to be lent to someone else — which is exactly what we do with it.

The evidence

What the research actually found

In 2015 the American Economic Journal: Applied Economics published six randomised evaluations of microcredit conducted in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Ethiopia, India, Mexico, Mongolia, and Morocco, alongside a joint analysis by Abhijit Banerjee, Dean Karlan, and Jonathan Zinman. It remains the most serious body of evidence on the question.

What it supported

Access to microcredit consistently increased business investment and the scale of existing enterprises. Borrowers used the loans much as intended: buying stock, equipment, and inputs. Households gained flexibility in how they managed money, and there was no evidence of the widespread harm critics had predicted.

What it did not support

The studies found no consistent evidence that microcredit transformed average household income, consumption, health, schooling, or women’s empowerment. Take-up was also lower than expected — many eligible households simply did not want a loan. Microcredit is a useful financial tool, not a cure for poverty.

The honest summary is that microcredit reliably does a modest thing well, and does not do the extraordinary thing it was once promoted as doing.

Our reading of the 2015 evidence

See Banerjee, Karlan & Zinman, “Six Randomized Evaluations of Microcredit: Introduction and Further Steps”, AEJ: Applied Economics, 2015; and J-PAL’s policy summary on microcredit’s impacts and limitations.

The hard questions

Criticisms we take seriously

We would rather answer these directly than have someone discover them later and wonder why we did not mention them.

In most cases, yes. The field partners that originate loans locally have real costs — loan officers who travel to villages, offices, currency risk, defaults — and they charge interest to cover them. Rates are usually well above what a borrower in a wealthy country would pay, and well below what local moneylenders charge, which is often the actual alternative.

We think the honest framing is this: microfinance is not free money. It is credit at a price that is defensible relative to the alternatives available to the borrower, and indefensible when a lender pushes it past that point. Choosing partners carefully is the whole job.

It can be, and it has been. The 2010 crisis in Andhra Pradesh, India, is the clearest case: rapid commercial expansion led to households holding several loans at once, aggressive collection practices, and severe harm. It prompted regulation and a serious reckoning across the sector.

The lesson is about how lending is done rather than whether it should be. Credit extended to someone with a productive use for it, on a schedule matched to their income, is a normal part of running a business. Credit pushed onto someone who is already over-borrowed is predatory. The randomised evidence found no sign of widespread harm from access to microcredit, but that finding depends on lending being conducted responsibly.

The lender absorbs the loss. Kiva reports a historical repayment rate of roughly 96% across its portfolio, which means a meaningful minority of loans are not repaid in full. Businesses fail, harvests fail, people get sick.

We plan for that rather than around it. When we report our results, defaults are counted as defaults, and our published figures will show the amount lost as well as the amount lent.

Closer to home

Small enterprise in Latin America

Financial inclusion in Latin America and the Caribbean has improved substantially — account ownership among adults rose from 39% in 2011 to 70% in 2024, and mobile money accounts reached 37%. That is real progress in a little over a decade.

The gap that remains is not primarily about opening accounts. It is about credit. Micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises make up roughly 99% of firms in the region and employ a large majority of the workforce, yet face a financing gap that the Inter-American Development Bank has estimated in the trillions. A business can have a bank account, steady customers, and still be unable to borrow $500 to expand.

That gap is precisely the shape of a microloan.
